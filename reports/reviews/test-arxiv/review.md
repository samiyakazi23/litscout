# Machine learning for flood forecasting and flood impact prediction in large river basins

*15 papers reviewed; 100% of cited sentences verified against their sources.*

## ML-based Hydrological Modeling

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks have been applied to streamflow simulation in the Limpopo River Basin using observed discharge, meteorological drivers, and catchment descriptors, but data sparsity limits performance [1]. An Entity‑Aware LSTM trained on 531 CAMELS basins outperforms both regionally calibrated and basin‑specific hydrological benchmarks, demonstrating the advantage of large‑sample training [8]. KNOU‑Flood extends a single‑layer LSTM with lead‑aware forecast inputs, two‑stage transfer learning, and additional forecast sources (GraphCast, ECMWF HRES), achieving a median KGE' of 0.65 at 1‑day lead and 0.48 at 10‑day lead, surpassing the AIFL baseline [11]. The Common Mechanism Regression (CMR) model leverages few in‑situ discharge measurements together with satellite observations to estimate river discharge across many locations, showing accurate simulation in synthetic experiments [14]. A physics‑embedded multi‑timescale learning model (dHBV2.0MTS‑MC) improves median hourly Nash‑Sutcliffe efficiency from 0.461 to 0.683 and reduces peak‑timing errors, illustrating the benefit of embedding hydrodynamic physics in data‑driven models [12].

## Deep Learning for Real-Time Flood Forecasting

Google's operational flood forecasting system employs LSTM networks for stage forecasting and linear models as baselines; the LSTM consistently shows higher skill, while Thresholding and Manifold models provide comparable inundation extent predictions [4]. A reachability‑based graph transformation densifies river network topology for Graph Neural Networks, enabling transformed GNNs to match EA‑LSTM 14‑hour water‑level accuracy with a 24‑hour horizon—a 71% improvement in predictive lead time [5]. FloodGTN integrates Graph Neural Networks, LSTM, and a Transformer to attend to heterogeneous covariates (rainfall, tide, hydraulic structures), delivering a 70% accuracy gain over the physics‑based HEC‑RAS model and reducing runtime by >500× [6]. APS‑LSTM introduces adaptive periodic and spatial self‑attention on top of LSTM, capturing multi‑periodic temporal patterns and diverse spatial dependencies, and outperforms existing flood prediction models on two real‑world datasets [9]. In the Kabul River basin, a suite of machine‑learning models (SVM, XGBoost, ANN, GRU, LSTM) was evaluated; the LSTM achieved the best performance with R² = 0.96 and RMSE = 140.96 m³/s for daily and multi‑step forecasts [2].

## ML for Flood Impact and Damage Assessment

An Extreme Gradient Boosting model trained on National Flood Insurance Program claims and Daymet rainfall estimates predicts flood‑induced financial damages in eight US Southwest coastal counties with R² = 0.807; bias correction and additional rainfall predictors further boost performance [15]. A cross‑modal fusion network predicts flood peak depth six hours before the event with a mean absolute error of 0.39 m by combining 5,689 high‑water‑mark surveys, Copernicus GLO‑30 terrain, AlphaEarth embeddings, and 144 h of hourly meteorological forcing across 24 US states and Puerto Rico [10].

## Remote Sensing and Data Assimilation in ML Flood Prediction

A physics‑guided deep learning framework merges UNet, Fourier Neural Operator, and physics‑informed residual losses to fuse Sentinel‑1 SAR, Sentinel‑2 optical imagery, DEM‑derived terrain features, and hydrodynamic simulation references; it attains an Intersection over Union of 0.82 and an F1 score of 0.90 for flood‑extent prediction, outperforming UNet‑only and FNO‑only baselines [3]. Another physics‑guided neural network incorporates Sentinel‑1 time‑series SAR images and local river gauge water‑elevation observations, using a Pearson correlation loss between predicted flood area and gauge levels; it achieves IoU = 0.89 for water and 0.96 for non‑water, surpassing unsupervised methods especially under low‑elevation conditions [7].

## Research gaps

- No studies address comprehensive flood impact metrics such as infrastructure damage, population displacement, or long‑term socioeconomic loss; existing work focuses on financial claims [15] and peak‑depth prediction [10].
- Real‑time assimilation of satellite‑derived flood extents into operational streamflow forecasts is missing; the closest efforts are physics‑guided extent models [3][7] and operational stage forecasting [4], but they are not integrated in a live forecasting loop.
- Probabilistic and uncertainty quantification for flood‑extent or inundation depth predictions is underexplored; only KNOU‑Flood provides an ensemble CRPS for streamflow [11], leaving a gap for spatial flood maps.
- Explainability of real‑time flood forecasts remains limited; CACD offers interpretable risk prediction [13] but does not extend to operational forecasting models such as those in [4][5][6][9].
- Transferability of models to data‑scarce, large transboundary basins (e.g., many African or Asian rivers) is insufficiently demonstrated; Limpopo study notes data constraints [1] and Kabul study highlights upstream data gaps [2].
- Integration of climate‑change scenario forecasting with ML‑based hydrological models is absent; none of the cited works evaluate model performance under future climate projections.
- End‑to‑end pipelines that combine streamflow forecasting, inundation mapping, and damage estimation in a single automated system are not presented; existing components exist separately in [4], [3][7], and [15].
